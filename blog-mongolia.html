Mongolia: four days in gers, three days on a Soviet motorbike

August 2018. I flew in from Singapore, my best friend François flew in from Vietnam, and neither of us had ever set foot in Mongolia. Here is roughly how we spent nine days, and what I would tell you to do differently.

The plan, in three parts

I was living in Singapore then, and getting to Mongolia started with a visa appointment at the Mongolian embassy there. François was based in Vietnam. We picked a date, booked separately, and met in Ulaanbaatar.

We split the trip three ways, which I still think is the right shape for a first visit:

  • Two days in Ulaanbaatar, just walking around the city.
  • Four days staying with herding families out west.
  • A motorbike trip east to Terelj. Two days for François, three for me.

The middle part was booked through a small outfit that a friend had used and recommended, somewhere between an NGO and a tour agency. What they do is arrange homestays with families on the steppe and give a large share of the money straight back to the families. That was the reason we picked them, and it is the reason the four days felt like visiting people rather than visiting a product.

Four days with herding families

We took a bus west out of Ulaanbaatar towards the old imperial capital, four or five hours across flat, dry country. A local herder picked us up at the other end and drove us to his ger.

That first family took us out on a camel ride at sunset, up to a small temple on a hill where food was laid out. Then we sat in the ger and ate and did very little, which turned out to be the correct way to spend an evening in Mongolia.

The second family lived beside a stream, with kids running around outside. This was the best of the four days. We rode horses out into open steppe with nobody and nothing in any direction, which is a feeling I have not had anywhere else. They also cooked us what they described as river rats, skinned and then cooked whole, and I will be honest with you: it was really, really good.

The third family were up on a hill, and we were not alone this time. There were Mongols visiting from China, and they had shot a mountain marmot. What they did next I only learned the name of later: they heated stones in a fire, put the hot stones inside the animal, and cooked it from the inside out. It is called boodog. If you get offered it, say yes.

Nothing happens until the sun goes down

Here is the thing nobody told us, and the single most useful thing I can pass on.

During the day, on the steppe, there is almost nothing to do. The sun is strong, the land is flat in every direction, and there is no list of activities waiting for you. You can walk. You can ride. That is the menu.

And then the sun starts dropping and the whole place switches on. With the second family we got on horses and helped bring the sheep back towards the camp, which was genuinely one of the best hours of the trip. The riding, the work, the light: all of it happens at the end of the day.

Come for the evenings. Learn to enjoy the afternoons.

Once I understood that, I stopped feeling like I was wasting time. I liked sitting in a ger with nothing scheduled. François read his books. Nobody needed entertaining.

Travelling with someone who travels differently

Mongolia is where I worked out that François and I travel in completely different ways. He is less drawn to the adventure side of it and much more intellectual than me, better read, and he comes alive around museums, history, and understanding how people actually live. I want to be on the horse.

It did not cause a single problem. We just got different things out of the same nine days, and both of us got a lot. If you are planning a trip with a friend whose instincts are not yours, that is worth knowing in advance rather than discovering on day four.

Terelj on a Soviet motorbike replica

Back in Ulaanbaatar we found a Mongolian woman, married to a Frenchman, who rents out old Soviet-design motorbikes built in China. We took two and rode east to Gorkhi-Terelj National Park.

The first night we stayed in a ger with a former colonel in the Mongolian army. He spoke English, which out there is a small miracle, and he brought vodka and stories, in that order. One of the best evenings I have had anywhere.

Then François rode back to the city and I stayed out one more day. It rained the whole time. Late on, I found a ger in the middle of nowhere and asked a young family if I could stay. They said yes. I slept on the floor, ate what they ate, and helped with the cows in the morning before riding back to Ulaanbaatar.

I flew out that evening, with just enough time to get to the big market in the middle of the city first. Boots, tools, saddles, coats, everything. Rough, loud, brilliant. Go, and keep a hand on your bag.

What I would tell you to do

  • Book the homestay part through an operator that pays the families directly. It costs about the same and it changes what the days feel like.
  • Plan for the evenings, not the days. Riding, herding and eating all happen late. Bring a book for the afternoons and stop fighting it.
  • Say yes to the food. All of it.
  • Give yourself an extra day at the end with no plan. My best night of the trip was the one I had not arranged, in a stranger's ger, in the rain.
  • Sort the visa before you fly. Mine took an embassy appointment in Singapore, so check the rules for wherever you are actually flying from, not for your passport country.

This was my first time in Mongolia and my first time on those steppes. I went back in 2024 on a work trip and had a great time, but it was a different kind of trip. The 2018 version, nine days, three families and a rented motorbike, is the one I would do again tomorrow.
