A day job and a thirteen-year record

In June 2013 I walked out of Le Puy-en-Velay and kept going for two months, all the way to Santiago de Compostela. Six weeks later I was in Ho Chi Minh City, buying a motorbike I had learned to ride three days earlier, then riding it to Hanoi and back. Forty days, sleeping wherever people would have me.

Everything since has followed that shape. On Mont Blanc in 2015, the glacier training day on the Mer de Glace was the first time I had ever worn crampons. Five days later I was standing on the summit at dawn.

The record I'm proudest of includes the two times I turned around.

Aconcagua stopped me at 6,500 m in January 2025. The Matterhorn's Hörnli Ridge stopped me at roughly 3,800 m that August. Twelve months later I summited Kilimanjaro, then climbed and skied Yotei and Fuji.

The rest of the week I work in tech at Google, in Tokyo. That is not a footnote. It is why the planning is thorough, why deadlines get met, why a brief comes back on time and why the debrief is written rather than promised. Every one of these trips was built around a full-time job, which is also why the record is steady rather than spectacular: one objective at a time, for thirteen years.

Being based in Tokyo puts the Japanese mountains an early train away. Five of the sixteen are here.
