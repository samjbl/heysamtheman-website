Two days on Mt. Yotei: one turnaround, one summit

February 2026, Hokkaido. We booked a guide for two days because we were not confident we would get up on the first one. We did not. That decision is the entire reason this post has a summit in it.

Why you book two days on Yotei

Six of us went, all friends from work in Tokyo, including Yoshi and Shin, and Shin organised the whole thing. He found a Japanese guide rather than one of the Western outfits in Niseko, which is noticeably cheaper, and he booked two days instead of one.

That was the best decision of the trip. Yotei sits on its own above the Niseko valley with nothing around it to break the weather, so it gets whatever the sky is doing that morning, at full strength. One booked day is one roll of the dice.

We actually ended up with a different guide on each day: an older man on the first, a younger one on the second. Same meeting point, same car park, completely different mountain.

Day one: turned back above the tree line

We met around 7am. It was snowing, it was cold, and the wind was up. None of us knew the terrain yet, so we were reading the place as we climbed.

We got a little above the tree line and stopped. It was late in the day by then, the weather was not improving, and one of the group was struggling badly. We turned around.

It was the right call and it was not a close one. And here is the part people skip when they write about turnarounds: the descent was excellent. The snow quality was superb. We did not summit and we still had a great day on the mountain, which is more or less the deal Hokkaido offers you in February.

The gear problem that made the call for us

One of our group had two problems at once, and they compounded.

First, his splitboard bindings were already failing before we started. It was a cheap setup and it was not holding, so every transition cost him energy and time he did not have.

Second, he was wearing one very thick coat instead of layers. On a skin track in cold weather that is the classic mistake. You overheat, you soak the inside of the coat with sweat, and then the moment you stop moving you are wet and freezing. There is no way to fix it on the hill.

He had also, honestly, overestimated what he could do that day. All three things together stopped the group. Not the weather on its own.

Test the kit at home. The mountain is a bad place to find out your bindings are broken.

He made the right decision the next morning and sat day two out.

Day two: the summit

Sunny. Still windy, so the weather was not perfect, but we could see the sun and feel it, which on Yotei in February counts as a good day.

Five of us went up. It took somewhere around five to six hours to the top. The climb is a long, steady, relentless skin up a cone, and the reward is that you are standing on one of the most recognisable mountains in Japan looking down over the whole Niseko valley.

We took a lot of photos at the top and then took our time coming down, because the snow was still really, really good and there was no reason to rush it.

It was special for the obvious reasons. It is an iconic mountain, it was tough but achievable, and we did it with a group of good friends. Getting to ski something like that is a privilege and I am not going to pretend otherwise. This is what I do it for.

What a guide actually costs, and why we used one

Roughly ¥100,000 per day. Split six ways that is manageable, but it is still real money, and it changes how you think about the trip.

It cut both ways for us. Knowing what day two was costing is part of why we were so committed to getting up. But it is also exactly why booking two days is the right call rather than the expensive one: a single day that the weather takes from you is a total loss, and the second day is the only thing that turns a cancelled trip into a summit.

Planning notes if you are going

  • Book two days. The cost per summit is lower than one day and a coin flip.
  • A Japanese guide is meaningfully cheaper than a foreign-run outfit in Niseko for the same mountain.
  • Layer properly. It is a long, warm climb in cold air. One big coat will ruin your day.
  • Check your bindings and your skins before you fly. Especially splitboard hardware.
  • A turnaround is not a wasted day here. The skiing on the way down is the reason people come to Hokkaido in the first place.

What it really did was give us the appetite for more. Rishiri, up off the north coast of Hokkaido, is the next one on the list, and everyone who has done it says it is a proper adventure. There is so much terrain up there that this could go on indefinitely, which suits me fine.
