Walking Japan's Shikoku 88-temple pilgrimage (O-Henro): stages, lodging & pack weight

How to walk Japan's 1,200 km Shikoku 88-temple circuit without destroying your knees on coastal tarmac or missing the 17:00 temple stamp cutoff. Verified prefecture stages, updated ¥500 stamp fees, shukubō vs. minshuku costs, footwear choices and seasonal rain planning.

Most guides describe the Shikoku 88-Temple Pilgrimage (O-Henro) in purely spiritual terms, then leave walkers surprised by its real physical demands. Completing the loop on foot means covering roughly 1,200 kilometres across four prefectures where around 85% of the mileage sits on hard asphalt and 15% climbs steep, root-bound mountain trails known as henro-korogashi.

Having walked the Shikoku O-Henro in June 2014, the core logistical reality has held steady while prices have moved: a full thru-walk takes 45 to 60 days at 20 to 30 kilometres a day, stamp fees rose to ¥500 per temple in 2024, and finishing smoothly depends less on brute fitness than on matching your footwear, pack weight and daily 17:00 stamp-office discipline to Shikoku's terrain.

01Route architecture

1. Getting to Temple 1 (Ryōzen-ji) & pacing the four prefectures

Osaka to TokushimaAwa Express highway bus ~2h45 (~¥4,100); direct buses from KIX too
Last-mile railJR Kōtoku Line Tokushima to Bandō (20–30 mins, ~¥390), 10-min walk
Continuous pace45–50 days comfortable (20–30 km/day); ~40 days pushing 30+ km
Watch out forGolden Week (late Apr–early May) inn sellouts; section-walking (kugiri-uchi) works too

Most international walkers reach Temple 1, Ryōzen-ji in Naruto, via Osaka or Kansai International Airport (KIX). Catching the highway bus straight to Tokushima Station takes roughly 2 hours 45 minutes (~¥4,100 from Osaka), followed by a 20 to 30 minute ride on the JR Kōtoku Line to Bandō Station (~¥390) and a ten-minute signposted walk to the temple gate. Ryōzen-ji's shop sells the full pilgrim kit (white hakui vest, sugegasa sedge hat, kongō-zue staff and blank nōkyōchō stamp book) for roughly ¥15,000 to ¥30,000 if you choose to carry traditional attire.

From Bandō, the traditional clockwise loop moves through four distinct prefectural chapters: Tokushima (Temples 1–23) mixes level river valleys with your first tough climbs; Kōchi (Temples 24–39) stretches along vast Pacific Ocean highways where days can pass between temples; Ehime (Temples 40–65) weaves through orchard valleys, coastal capes and Matsuyama; and Kagawa (Temples 66–88) packs the final twenty-three temples tightly together down to Temple 88, Ōkubo-ji. If you do not have two full months off work, splitting the walk into one- or two-week blocks (kugiri-uchi) is completely standard.

Bottom lineTake the highway bus to Tokushima and local JR train to Bandō, then plan conservative 20–25 km daily blocks through Tokushima before stretching your legs on Kōchi's coastal flats.
02Crux climbs

2. Respecting Shikoku's three signature mountain temples (nansho)

Shōsan-ji (Temple 12)First major Tokushima mountain ordeal via steep cedar trails
Yokomine-ji (Temple 60)Roughly 740 m up the slopes of Mt. Ishizuchi in Ehime
Unpen-ji (Temple 66)Highest circuit temple (~910 m) on the Tokushima/Kagawa border
Watch out forWet tree roots and steep mud on henro-korogashi descents after rain

Although Shikoku is mostly lowland roads and rural lanes, a handful of remote mountain sanctuaries called nansho (hard-to-reach places) deliver proper mountain elevation days. The first shock arrives early at Temple 12 (Shōsan-ji) in Tokushima, where a long cedar-forest climb known as a henro-korogashi (literally "where pilgrims tumble down") tests every early-stage walker's calves and pack straps.

Later in the loop, Temple 60 (Yokomine-ji) climbs to roughly 740 metres on the steep wooded flank of Mt. Ishizuchi, western Japan's highest mountain, before Temple 66 (Unpen-ji) reaches the circuit's highest elevation at roughly 910 metres near the Tokushima–Kagawa border. Carrying a kongō-zue wooden staff or collapsible trekking poles protects your knees on the long, rocky descents off these passes, and tired walkers can ride the Unpenji Ropeway down or up if a storm rolls in.

Bottom lineTreat Shōsan-ji, Yokomine-ji and Unpen-ji as genuine mountain days with early starts, extra water and trekking poles or your staff.
03Temple fees & cash

3. Updated ¥500 stamp fees, 17:00 cutoffs & total circuit budget

Stamp fee (nōkyō)¥500 per temple (¥44,000 total); ¥300 repeat visit; ¥200 hakui vest
Office hours07:00–17:00 standard (some open 08:00; grounds open ~06:00)
Coin offeringsRoughly ¥15,000–¥18,000 total across 88 Main + Daishi halls
Watch out forZero credit cards at rural stamp desks and strict 17:00 shutters

For decades the calligraphy and red-ink seal at each temple cost ¥300, which is why many older blogs still understate the bill. Since the 2024 price revision, every first-time nōkyōchō stamp costs ¥500 per temple, putting the stamp fee alone at ¥44,000 across all 88 temples (plus ¥2,000 to ¥3,500 for the blank accordion book). Factor in small coin offerings (osaisen) at both the Main Hall and Daishi Hall of each temple and cash temple expenses sit around ¥60,000 before lodging or food.

Timing matters as much as cash. Temple stamp offices (nōkyōjo) run strictly on daylight hours, typically 07:00 to 17:00 (with a few smaller sites opening at 08:00 or pausing briefly around midday). If you reach a gate at 17:05, the desk is shut until morning, which can wreck the next day's stage schedule. Carry plenty of ¥100 and ¥500 coins and ¥1,000 notes, because remote temples and rural vending machines rarely take cards.

Bottom lineBudget ¥44,000 for stamps plus ~¥16,000 in coins, carry small cash notes daily, and plan every walking day to clear your last temple before 16:45.
04Sleep & hospitality

4. Shukubō temple stays vs. minshuku inns vs. henro huts

Minshuku / Ryokan¥7,000–¥9,000/night with dinner & breakfast (~¥2,000 less room-only)
Shukubō (Temples)¥8,000–¥10,000/night with two meals + morning/evening prayers
Budget optionsHenro Houses (~¥4,500 room-only), zenkonyado & free henro-goya huts
Watch out forMany rural minshuku accept Japanese phone bookings only

Accommodation defines both your daily budget and how heavy a pack you carry. Staying at family-run minshuku (¥7,000–¥9,000 with hot dinner and early breakfast) or temple shukubō (¥8,000–¥10,000 with two meals and optional morning chanting) lets you skip carrying a tent, sleeping pad or stove altogether. Most inns wash and dry pilgrim clothes for a few hundred yen and hand out rice-ball lunches on request, pushing a standard two-month circuit budget to roughly ¥400,000–¥600,000.

Walkers on a tighter ¥200,000–¥350,000 budget mix room-only sudomari guesthouses (~¥4,500), altruistic local zenkonyado stays, and free wooden roadside shelters (henro-goya), refuelling from supermarkets and convenience stores. Whether you stay in a temple or a hut, expect Shikoku's tradition of osettai: locals handing walkers fruit, canned coffee, snacks or small cash bills along the road. Carry small name slips (osame-fuda) to offer in polite return.

Bottom lineBook minshuku and shukubō with meals to keep your pack light, or mix henro huts and sudomari rooms if you want to cut costs below ¥350,000.
05Kit & foot care

5. Why cushioned trail runners & an under-8 kg pack beat stiff boots

Surface breakdown~85% hard asphalt/concrete roads · ~15% mountain footpaths
Footwear pickCushioned trail runners, sized 0.5–1 full size up for foot swell
Target base weight6–8 kg without tent; 8–10 kg if sleeping in henro huts
Watch out forHeavy B1/B2 leather boots frying joints and Gore-Tex liners trapping sweat

New pilgrims frequently arrive in Shikoku wearing rigid leather hiking boots meant for alpine gravel, then spend week two nursing shin splints and deep heel blisters. Pounding 25 kilometres of flat Japanese tarmac day after day punishes stiff soles far harder than a mountain trail does. Cushioned trail running shoes with grippy lugs handle both the 85% road mileage and the 15% muddy forest climbs, especially if bought 0.5 to 1 full size larger to absorb multi-week foot swelling.

Think carefully before choosing waterproof Gore-Tex uppers. In light morning dew they keep socks dry, yet once monsoon rain runs down your shins or fills a shoe on a flooded lane, waterproof membranes trap water inside and take two days to dry. Breathable non-waterproof mesh paired with merino or synthetic toe-socks dries quickly as you walk. Keep your base pack under 7–8 kg so 1,200 kilometres never turns into a knee rehab project.

Bottom lineWear well-cushioned, half-size-up trail runners with quick-dry merino socks, and keep your base pack strictly below 8 kg.
06Timing & weather

6. Best walking windows (and surviving June tsuyu rain)

Prime spring windowMarch to April (mild air, blossoms; avoid Golden Week rush)
Prime autumn windowOctober to November (drier air, stable temps, mountain colour)
Summer / monsoon risksJune tsuyu downpours; July–August heatstroke; September typhoons
Watch out forExposed, shadeless Pacific asphalt in Kōchi during hot spells

On paper, March–April and October–November are the undisputed prime windows for walking Shikoku. Daytime temperatures stay comfortable for 25-kilometre marches, rain totals drop in autumn, and mountain foliage around Shōsan-ji and Unpen-ji is crisp without winter snow hazards. If walking in spring, book Tokushima and Matsuyama rooms early around Japan's Golden Week (late April into early May), when domestic travellers fill every coastal inn.

Walking in June — as I did in 2014 — drops you straight into Japan's tsuyu rainy season, trading crowds for heavy humidity, slick mountain roots and daily downpours. Mid-summer (July and August) is harsher still on unshaded Kōchi highways, while September carries peak typhoon risk. If your schedule forces a June start, pack a ventilated full-body rain poncho that covers your backpack, a compact folding umbrella for warm drizzle, and spare dry socks sealed inside a drybag.

Bottom lineTarget March–April or October–November for dry footing and steady temperatures, reserving June only if you pack a full-pack poncho and accept wet mountain descents.

One habit that quietly rescues almost every first-time henro: map your next two nights of lodging every evening before dinner, especially along Kōchi's sparse southern coast between Temples 24 and 37. Rural Japanese inns often close check-in by 18:00 and buy fresh fish strictly for booked guests, so locking tomorrow's bed in early leaves you free to walk at your own rhythm without racing the 17:00 stamp shutters.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to walk the entire Shikoku 88-temple pilgrimage?

Most walkers finish the roughly 1,200 km circular route in 45 to 60 days at 20 to 30 km per day. Fast hikers pushing 30+ km daily can complete it in around 40 days, while many walkers split it into shorter 1-to-2-week section trips (kugiri-uchi) across multiple visits.

How much does walking the Shikoku 88 cost in 2026–2027?

A standard inn-supported thru-walk staying in minshuku and shukubō with dinner and breakfast costs roughly ¥400,000 to ¥600,000+ (¥7,000–¥10,000/night plus ¥44,000 for 88 temple stamps at ¥500 each and ~¥15,000–¥18,000 in coin offerings). Budget walkers using room-only guesthouses, henro huts and supermarkets typically spend ¥200,000 to ¥350,000.

Did Shikoku temple stamp fees increase from ¥300 to ¥500?

Yes. Each nōkyōchō calligraphy and red-ink stamp at the 88 temples now costs ¥500 for a first visit (totalling ¥44,000 across the circuit). Subsequent visits with the same stamp book (kasane-in) cost ¥300 per temple, and stamping a white hakui vest costs ¥200.

What are the best shoes to wear for the Shikoku pilgrimage?

Cushioned trail running shoes or light walking shoes sized 0.5 to 1 full size larger than everyday shoes work best. Around 85% of the route follows hard asphalt and concrete roads where rigid leather mountaineering boots cause unnecessary joint fatigue and blisters, while trail-runner grip handles the 15% steep mountain passes.
