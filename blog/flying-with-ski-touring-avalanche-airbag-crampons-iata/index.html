Flying with an avalanche airbag, ice axes & lithium gear: IATA & Japan airport rules

How to get ski touring and alpine hardware through international and Japanese airport security without having cylinders, batteries or sharp kit confiscated at check-in. Exact IATA Table 2.3.A rules, Japan MLIT power bank caps, supercapacitor vs compressed-gas trade-offs, and checked-bag packing rules.

Every winter, skiers and alpinists lose trips at the check-in desk because airline dangerous-goods rules treat an avalanche airbag, a lithium power bank and a pair of crampons under three completely separate rulebooks. One wrong packing decision puts a spare battery in hold luggage (instant bag pull) or traps a pressurised gas canister at a US transit hub or a regional Japanese counter.

This guide breaks down the exact rules governing commercial flights to winter hubs like Haneda, Narita, New Chitose and Geneva under IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations (Table 2.3.A), US TSA screening and Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) directives enforced by JAL and ANA.

01Zero-paperwork air travel choice

1. Alpride E2 supercapacitor airbags (Scott, Osprey, BCA)

Best forFrequent flyers connecting across US, European & Japanese carriers
Energy & weight1,140 g system weight · Supercapacitors <0.3 Wh (+ optional 2x AA cells)
Baggage statusPermitted in carry-on or checked luggage with no dangerous-goods limits
Watch out forKeep optional AA cells protected; higher upfront pack cost than canister systems

If you fly repeatedly for ski touring, pure supercapacitor systems simplify airport logistics more than any paperwork workaround. The Alpride E2 compressor unit uses electric double-layer capacitors storing less than 0.3 Wh of chemical-free electrical energy, well below the 10 Wh aviation threshold for capacitors, with zero pressurised gas cylinders and zero pyrotechnic squibs.

Because the pack carries no high-capacity lithium-ion battery inside the main compressor (recharging in roughly 20 minutes via USB-C or 40 minutes off two user-replaceable AA batteries), it sidesteps both compressed-gas carrier approvals and lithium battery watt-hour limits. On multi-leg itineraries into Hokkaido or regional Alps airports, simply switch the unit off and store the pull handle.

Bottom lineThe cleanest hardware choice for frequent winter flyers who want zero gas-cartridge or lithium-wattage friction at check-in.
02Ultralight hybrid electric system

2. Ortovox Avabag LiTRIC & Arc'teryx Micon LiTRIC

Best forWeight-conscious ski mountaineers flying internationally
Energy & weight~1,080–1,100 g base unit · 7.4 Wh (3.7V / 2,000 mAh) Li-ion + <0.3 Wh caps
Baggage statusApproved under 100 Wh installed-battery PED rules (power unit OFF)
Watch out forCheck-in staff may assume any avalanche bag holds gas unless you show docs

The LiTRIC system shaves roughly 40 to 60 grams off pure capacitor setups by pairing supercapacitors with a permanent internal 7.4 Wh lithium-ion battery (roughly half the energy of a standard smartphone battery). It recharges via USB-C in around 25 minutes and handles multiple practice deployments without buying replacement canisters.

Under IATA, JAL and ANA rules, a 7.4 Wh installed lithium-ion battery sits comfortably below the 100 Wh threshold for portable electronic devices. Power the pack completely off rather than leaving it in standby, secure the handle, and keep a printed copy of the manufacturer datasheet inside the top lid so X-ray operators recognise it immediately as a low-wattage electric fan unit.

Bottom lineMarginally lighter than pure capacitor packs while remaining well clear of the 100 Wh aviation battery threshold.
03High-power Li-ion fan system

3. Black Diamond & PIEPS JetForce Pro

Best forMulti-deployment ski touring without compressed gas or AA cells
Energy & weight57.6 Wh Li-ion battery (older classic JetForce units are 43.2 Wh)
Baggage statusPermitted under IATA <100 Wh rules; carry-on strongly recommended
Watch out forLarger visible Li-ion battery pack frequently triggers manual hold-bag checks

Unlike LiTRIC's small 7.4 Wh cell, JetForce Pro drives its fan directly off a 57.6 Wh lithium-ion pack. That still falls cleanly under the 100 Wh threshold set by IATA, TSA, JAL and ANA, and removes all compressed-gas cylinder headaches.

In practice, however, flying with a JetForce pack as your carry-on bag avoids delays. A 57.6 Wh cell looks substantial on hold-luggage X-rays, and baggage screeners unfamiliar with electric avalanche bags may pull the ski duffel for manual inspection. Disarm the system completely so all status LEDs are off, confirm the printed 57.6 Wh label is readable, and carry the Black Diamond or PIEPS travel sheet with your boarding pass.

Bottom lineFully compliant under 100 Wh rules, but pack it in the cabin so security never pulls your checked ski bag.
04Strictest paperwork & Japan refill hurdle

4. Compressed-gas cylinder airbags (Mammut RAS 3.0 & BCA Float)

Best forSkiers already owning cylinder packs who pre-arrange local refills
Gas specIATA Table 2.3.A: 1 pack/person, Div 2.2 gas, pressure-relief valve, <=200 mg 1.4S trigger
Baggage statusAirline approval mandatory; TSA bans pressurised cylinders on US routes
Watch out forSpare canisters are banned; Japanese dive shops cannot refill foreign cylinders

On paper, IATA Table 2.3.A permits one avalanche rescue backpack per passenger containing a Division 2.2 non-flammable, non-toxic gas cylinder (and up to 200 mg of Division 1.4S pyrotechnic trigger) in either checked or carry-on baggage, provided the airbags have pressure-relief valves and the airline grants prior approval. Spare cylinders are not permitted, and the cylinder must be disconnected or secured against accidental deployment.

Two regional realities catch travellers off guard. First, if your route starts or connects in the United States, TSA rules override IATA and ban pressurised cylinders completely: you must discharge the tank, unscrew the valve head, and pack both open in clear bags for inspection. Second, Japan enforces strict High Pressure Gas Safety laws. Ordinary dive shops or paintball centres in Japan are legally prohibited from filling foreign DOT or CE cylinders. If you fly to Niseko or Hakuba with an empty cylinder, reserve a local rental or exchange canister through an authorised backcountry shop before you depart.

Bottom lineWorkable on direct non-US flights with printed IATA Table 2.3.A approval, but much clumsier than electric packs if flying via the US or into Japan.
05Zero-tolerance cabin ban

5. Ice axes, crampons, ski poles & avalanche hardware

Best forChecked ski rollers and expedition duffels
Sharp kit ruleAxes, crampons, ski poles & metal avalanche shovels must be checked
Cabin priorityKeep avalanche beacon (transceiver), ski boots & goggles in carry-on
Watch out forUnprotected crampon points shred Gore-Tex shells and cut baggage handlers

IATA, TSA and Japan's JCAB/MLIT rules draw an unambiguous line on sharp mountaineering and touring hardware: ice axes, crampons, ski poles, pointed trekking poles and metal-blade avalanche shovels are prohibited in cabin baggage. Even short ultralight ski-mountaineering axes and folded carbon poles get confiscated at Haneda, Narita or European security lanes if left in a daypack.

Pack all sharp metal inside your checked ski bag or expedition duffel with rubber pick and adze guards on the axe, and house crampons inside a dedicated rigid case or thick protective pouch so twelve steel points never puncture your hardshell or airbag bladder under baggage-cart compression. Keep your avalanche transceiver in your carry-on (with batteries removed or switched fully off) alongside your custom-fitted ski boots so a delayed ski bag never ruins day one.

Bottom lineCheck every blade, spike and pole with proper point guards, and keep your transceiver and ski boots with you in the cabin.
06Updated Japan cabin & MLIT rules

6. Power banks, spare lithium batteries & Japan's 2-unit cap

Capacity limitsUp to 100 Wh freely allowed; 100–160 Wh max 2 with airline approval; >160 Wh banned
Japan MLIT capMax 2 power banks per passenger total; strictly NO overhead-bin storage
In-flight banProhibited to charge devices from a power bank or recharge the power bank onboard
Watch out forPower banks missing a printed Wh label risk confiscation at Asian checkpoints

Every spare lithium-ion battery and power bank (including spare action-camera cells and headlamp packs) is strictly forbidden in checked baggage worldwide. In Japan, MLIT safety directives enforced by JAL, ANA and all carriers operating Japanese routes go considerably further than standard IATA guidelines: following the July 2025 overhead-bin ban and the stricter rules effective April 24, 2026, passengers are limited to a maximum of two power banks per person, each rated at 160 Wh or less.

Crucially, Japan's onboard rules prohibit stowing power banks in overhead compartments and ban using them during the flight. Keep your power banks in a seatback pocket, your jacket or an under-seat bag where crew can spot overheating immediately, tape or pouch exposed battery terminals against short-circuits, and do not plug a power bank into your phone or into the aircraft seat outlet mid-flight. Charge devices directly from the aircraft seat USB/AC port instead, and verify that every pack has its Wh rating printed clearly on the casing (for example, 20,000 mAh at 3.7V equals 74 Wh).

Bottom lineCarry at most two clearly labelled sub-100 Wh power banks under your seat, fully charged before boarding, and leave them unplugged in the air.

Before locking your ski duffel, run a 60-second two-pile audit on the floor: anything with a point, blade or shaft goes in the checked hold; anything with a spare lithium cell or a transceiver goes under your seat. Keep printed IATA Table 2.3.A and manufacturer airbag sheets slipped inside the top pocket of your pack so an unfamiliar check-in agent never turns into a missed connection.

Frequently asked questions

Can I fly to Japan on JAL or ANA with an electric avalanche airbag like Alpride E2 or Ortovox LiTRIC?

Yes. The Alpride E2 uses supercapacitors storing under 0.3 Wh with no internal lithium-ion pack or pressurised gas, while the Ortovox LiTRIC uses a 7.4 Wh lithium-ion battery well below the 100 Wh limit. Switch the unit completely off, secure the pull handle against accidental activation, and carry the manufacturer datasheet in case check-in staff confuse it with a compressed-gas bag.

Can I pack a compressed-gas avalanche cylinder in my checked bag when flying via the US or to Japan?

Only under strict conditions. Under IATA Table 2.3.A, one avalanche backpack with a Division 2.2 gas cylinder and pressure-relief valve is allowed with prior airline approval (no spare cylinders). However, US TSA rules ban pressurised cylinders completely, requiring you to discharge the tank, unscrew the valve head and pack both open for inspection. In Japan, ordinary dive shops cannot legally refill foreign cylinders, so reserve a rental or swap cylinder locally in Niseko or Hakuba.

What are Japan's latest rules for flying with power banks on JAL and ANA?

Under Japan MLIT rules enforced across JAL, ANA and flights operating to or from Japan (including the overhead-bin ban from July 2025 and expanded restrictions effective April 24, 2026), passengers may carry a maximum of two power banks per person, each 160 Wh or less (100–160 Wh requires airline approval). Power banks are banned from checked bags and overhead bins, must stay under your seat or on your person, and cannot be used to charge devices or be recharged from seat outlets during the flight.

Do crampons, ice axes and ski poles have to go in checked luggage?

Yes. Ice axes, crampons, ski poles, pointed trekking poles and metal avalanche shovels are prohibited in cabin baggage under IATA, TSA and Japan MLIT security rules. Pack them in checked luggage with rubber guards or a rigid case over all picks and points, and keep your avalanche transceiver, ski boots and spare lithium batteries in your carry-on.
