Hiking Corsica's GR20: 7 lessons from Europe's roughest long-distance ridge trail

How to survive Corsica's 180 km granite spine from Calenzana to Conca without wrecking your knees or missing a hut bed. Verified PNRC refuge rates, North vs. South terrain splits, the Pointe des Éboulis bypass, pack weight ceilings and trailhead transport.

Most European long-distance trails ease you in along valley tracks before climbing a pass. Corsica's GR20 drops you straight onto steep, jagged granite from Stage 1 out of Calenzana, stacks roughly 12,000 metres of cumulative ascent across 180 kilometres, and punishes heavy packs and late morning starts harder than any Alpine hut-to-hut walk.

Having completed the full traverse in July 2021, the pattern is clear: fit hikers rarely quit the GR20 because of raw cardiovascular fatigue. They bail because 12 kg backpacks shred their knees on steep granite slabs, because they underestimate July afternoon heat and ridge storms, or because they treat the central railway town of Vizzavona as an afterthought instead of a tactical reset.

01Route architecture

1. Treat the North and South as two different sports

Classic split16 stages total (9 North to Vizzavona, 7 South to Conca)
Direction choiceNorth to South puts the hardest scrambling in week one
Midpoint rail linkVizzavona station connects Ajaccio, Corte and Bastia
Watch out forJudging daily effort by kilometres instead of vertical metres

Looking at a flat map, stages of 10 to 14 kilometres in the GR20 North (Calenzana to Vizzavona) look short. On the ground, you spend hours picking lines across tilted granite slabs, talus bowls and chained rock steps where sustained pace drops below 2.5 km/h. Walking North to South gets the most technical scrambling done while your legs and focus are fresh, though the first three days out of Calenzana are unforgiving.

Once you cross the railway tracks at Vizzavona, the character shifts noticeably. Apart from the optional Alpine variant along the Monte Incudine and Aiguilles de Bavella crests, the Southern half opens into wider ridge paths and beech forest where strong hikers routinely link two official stages into one day. Going South to North works well if you want four days to build trail legs before hitting the granite walls around Asco and Cinto.

Bottom linePlan North stages around 1,000 m vertical blocks and keep Vizzavona as a built-in bailout or resupply reset.
02Stage 4 & high point

2. Know the Pointe des Éboulis high route (Cirque de la Solitude is closed)

Current highest colPointe des Éboulis (2,607 m) between Haut-Asco and Tighjettu
Vertical climb~1,200 m ascent from Haut-Asco valley
Optional side summitMonte Cinto (2,706 m), +1.5 to 2 hours round trip
Watch out forOutdated guidebooks still routing through the closed Cirque

Older trip reports still describe the infamous chains and ladders of the Cirque de la Solitude on Stage 4. That gorge was permanently closed and de-equipped by the Parc Naturel Régional de Corse following a fatal landslide during a violent storm in June 2015. Do not follow faded paint marks or legacy GPX tracks into the old bowl.

The official GR20 now climbs high above Haut-Asco via steep granite slabs and loose scree to cross Pointe des Éboulis at 2,607 metres, now the highest point on the main trail, before traversing toward Bocca Crucetta and dropping to Refuge de Tighjettu. In clear weather, you can stash packs near the col and tag Monte Cinto (2,706 m), Corsica's highest peak, in under two hours return. In June, hard snow patches often linger on this pass long after the valleys feel like summer.

Bottom lineCheck snow conditions at Haut-Asco in early summer and verify you have a post-2016 GPX track loaded offline.
03Permits & sleeping

3. Book PNRC refuges and bivouac pitches early (no wild camping)

Booking portalpnr-resa.corsica (official PNRC system)
2026 PNRC rates€20 dorm bed · €12 own-tent pitch · €27 (1P) / €39 (2P) rental tent
Guarded windowMid-May to early October (varies slightly by hut)
Watch out forZero legal wild camping anywhere inside the park boundary

Wild camping is strictly forbidden across the GR20 corridor to protect fragile alpine grazing and reduce wildfire risk. Every overnight stop must be at an official PNRC refuge terrace, a private bergerie (sheepfold) with designated pitches, or a trailhead gîte. During the guarded season, advance reservation through pnr-resa.corsica is compulsory for both indoor dormitory bunks (bat-flanc, €20) and outdoor tent pitches (€12 if you bring your own tent).

If you want to travel fast without carrying a shelter, PNRC's pre-pitched rental tents (€27 for a solo pitch, €39 for a 2-person tent with mattresses) eliminate tent condensation and morning packing delays, while keeping you out of noisy shared dorms. Either way, you still need a light sleeping bag or liner and a headlamp for 05:00 departures.

Bottom lineLock in PNRC pitches weeks ahead for July and August, or pay €27 for a pre-pitched tent to keep your pack light.
04Kit & foot care

4. Hold your base pack under 8 kg and choose agile rock footwear

Target pack weight6–8 kg base weight (under 10 kg loaded with 2 L water)
Footwear pickVibram Megagrip trail runners or light approach shoes
Pole rulesCollapsible carbon/alloy poles with rubber tips for granite
Watch out forHeavy B1/B2 leather boots frying ankles on dry granite slabs

On a gentle valley trek, an extra 4 kg in your pack slows your flat pace slightly. Across 12,000 metres of steep Corsican descent, those extra kilos hammer your patellar tendons and turn technical down-climbs on slick slabs into sketchy balance tests. If you sleep in PNRC dorms or rental tents and eat hut dinners, a 30–38 litre pack under 8 kg is completely realistic.

Leave heavy rigid mountaineering boots at home unless you are crossing early-June snowfields that require microspikes. Low-cut trail runners or nimble approach shoes with sticky rubber outsoles (such as Vibram Megagrip) grip Corsican granite far better, dry overnight when soaked by river crossings or sweat, and save hundreds of grams per foot over 200,000+ steps.

Bottom lineEvery unnecessary kilogram costs you stability on wet granite and knee cartilage on the descents.
05Timing & weather

5. Start by 05:30 to beat ridge heat and afternoon storms

Ideal start time05:15–06:00 by head torch in July and August
Water carryMinimum 1.5–2 L capacity; filter all natural springs
Best windowsLate June (green, water-rich) or September (cooler, calmer)
Watch out forExposed ridges above 2,000 m after 14:00 in summer thunder

Corsica sits in the Mediterranean, so July and August combine intense reflected solar heat off pale granite with fast-building convective storms that roll over the central spine after 13:00. Leaving camp before 06:00 gets your steepest exposed passes finished in cool morning air and puts you in camp early enough to wash socks, nap and check the next day's water report.

Season timing drives water logistics just as much as heat. In June, snowmelt keeps streams flowing loudly on almost every col, though steep northern passes can still require microspikes. By late August and September, trail crowds thin out and daytime temperatures drop to ideal hiking levels, but low-elevation springs in the South dry up completely. Always ask the refuge gardien each morning which taps or streams are actually running.

Bottom lineAim for September if you want quiet trails and cool air, or late June if you want guaranteed water and long daylight.
06Budget & food

6. Carry cash euros for €20–€25 hut dinners and bergerie lunches

Daily hut food€20–€25 three-course dinner · €10–€13 breakfast
All-in daily budget€45–€65/day (bed or tent + hut dinner + breakfast + snacks)
Resupply pointsHaut-Asco, Castel di Vergio, Vizzavona, Bavella
Watch out forZero card terminals or unreliable signal at remote refuges

Unlike alpine huts in Switzerland or Austria that run tap-to-pay terminals, most Corsican refuges and private bergeries operate strictly on cash because satellite reception is patchy. Carry enough small euro notes (€10 and €20 bills) to cover your full stretch between road passes, since the nearest reliable ATMs sit down in Calvi, Corte, Ajaccio or Porto-Vecchio.

Refuge meals cannot be pre-booked on the central PNRC website; you register with the guardian upon arrival for dinner (usually pasta, Corsican lentil/charcuterie soup, or stew at €20–€25) and breakfast (€10–€13). Private bergeries along the route often serve much better local sheep cheese, omelettes and cold drinks at midday, letting you leave heavy stove canisters out of your pack altogether.

Bottom lineBudget €50–€65 per day in cash and trade stove weight for fresh bergerie stops where stages pass them.
07In & out logistics

7. Plan your Calenzana, Vizzavona and Conca transport before booking flights

North gatewayCalvi (airport/ferry) -> bus or 20 min taxi to Calenzana
Midpoint railChemins de Fer de la Corse train straight to Vizzavona
South gatewayConca -> shuttle/taxi to Sainte-Lucie or Porto-Vecchio / Figari
Watch out forSparse Sunday buses and shoulder-season timetable drops

The GR20 is a point-to-point traverse across an island with limited public transit, so open-jaw flights save hours of backtracking. Fly or take the ferry into Calvi (or Bastia via the scenic narrow-gauge train) for the northern trailhead at Calenzana, and depart from Figari-Sud Corse or Ajaccio after finishing in Conca.

Outside peak July and August, local buses linking Calvi to Calenzana or Porto-Vecchio to Conca run infrequently and often skip Sundays altogether. If your flight lands late, pre-book a local taxi or shared hiker shuttle through your trailhead gîte (such as Gîte La Tonnelle in Conca), and check corsicabus.org before locking in connections. If you only have one week, take the train straight from Ajaccio or Bastia to Vizzavona to hike either the technical North half or the faster South half.

Bottom lineBook open-jaw arrivals via Calvi and departures via Figari, and verify Sunday shuttle schedules in advance.

One final rule that saves more GR20 attempts than any piece of carbon gear: book an extra buffer day in Calvi or Porto-Vecchio before your return flight. Between thunderstorms that hold hikers down at a col and sparse island buses, building 24 hours of margin lets you finish the ridge safely instead of rushing down slick granite to catch a boarding gate.

Frequently asked questions

Is the GR20 harder than the Tour du Mont Blanc?

Yes, significantly. The Tour du Mont Blanc follows smooth alpine paths with valley villages and luggage transfers, whereas the GR20 covers ~180 km and ~12,000 m of ascent across steep granite slabs, loose scree and exposed ridges with hand-over-hand scrambling, especially in the North.

Can you wild camp on the GR20 in Corsica?

No. Wild camping is strictly illegal along the entire GR20 inside the Parc Naturel Régional de Corse. If you carry a tent, you must pitch inside the designated bivouac zones beside official PNRC refuges (€12 per pitch via pnr-resa.corsica) or approved private bergeries.

How much does the GR20 cost per day with PNRC refuges and meals?

Expect roughly €45 to €65 per day if you stay in PNRC facilities and buy hut food: €12 for an own-tent bivouac pitch or €20 for a refuge dorm bed (booked online in advance), plus €20–€25 for dinner and €10–€13 for breakfast paid in cash on arrival.

Can you hike only half of the GR20?

Yes. The Corsican railway runs directly through Vizzavona at the midpoint of the trail, connecting Ajaccio on the west coast with Corte and Bastia. You can take the train to Vizzavona in roughly one hour from Ajaccio to start the South half or exit after finishing the technical North half.
